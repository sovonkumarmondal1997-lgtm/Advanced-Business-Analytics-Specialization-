# Operations Analytics - Week 4, Session 1: Decision Trees and Decision Making Under Uncertainty

This session introduces **Decision Trees**, a powerful tool used to make decisions when future outcomes are uncertain. While previous weeks focused separately on optimization and simulation, this session combines decision-making with uncertainty. A decision tree helps managers visualize decisions, uncertain events, and their financial consequences so that different strategies can be compared systematically.

The session uses the example of **IDEA**, a Scandinavian furniture retailer planning to sell a new gazebo tent called **Krusbar** for €150 per unit. Demand is uncertain. There is a 50% chance of a **weak market** with demand of 5,000 units and a 50% chance of a **strong market** with demand of 10,000 units. IDEA must choose between two suppliers before demand becomes known. Supplier S can produce 5,000 units with no upfront fee but charges €120 per tent. Supplier P can produce 10,000 units, charges only €100 per tent, but requires a €50,000 upfront fee. IDEA can also choose not to launch the product.

A decision tree consists of three components:

* **Decision Nodes (Squares):** Points where a choice must be made.
* **Event Nodes (Circles):** Points where uncertain events occur.
* **Outcome Nodes (Triangles):** Final results, usually profits or costs.

The tree begins with IDEA choosing a supplier. After the supplier is selected, market demand becomes known, creating different profit outcomes. To calculate an outcome, all revenues and costs along a branch are added together. Probabilities at every event node must sum to 1.

For **Supplier S**, whether demand is weak or strong, IDEA can only supply 5,000 units. Therefore, profit is always:

* Revenue = 5,000 × €150
* Cost = 5,000 × €120
* Profit = **€150,000**

Thus Supplier S provides a guaranteed profit of €150,000.

For **Supplier P**, outcomes vary significantly:

* If demand is strong (10,000 units), profit = **€450,000**
* If demand is weak (5,000 units), profit = **–€300,000**

Supplier P offers higher potential reward but also substantial risk.

The session introduces three decision-making strategies:

### Maxi-Min Strategy (Risk-Averse)

Choose the option with the best worst-case outcome. This strategy focuses on avoiding losses.

* No Supplier → Worst outcome = €0
* Supplier S → Worst outcome = €150,000
* Supplier P → Worst outcome = –€300,000

**Best Choice: Supplier S** because it provides the highest minimum profit.

### Maxi-Max Strategy (Risk-Seeking)

Choose the option with the highest possible profit.

* No Supplier → Best outcome = €0
* Supplier S → Best outcome = €150,000
* Supplier P → Best outcome = €450,000

**Best Choice: Supplier P** because it offers the largest possible gain.

### Expected Value Strategy (Risk-Neutral)

Calculate the average profit using probabilities.

For Supplier P:

[
(0.5 \times 450,000) + (0.5 \times -300,000) = 75,000
]

For Supplier S:

[
(0.5 \times 150,000) + (0.5 \times 150,000) = 150,000
]

**Best Choice: Supplier S** because it has the higher expected profit.

The key takeaway is that decision trees provide a structured way to evaluate uncertain decisions. By combining decisions, probabilities, and financial outcomes, managers can identify risk-averse, risk-seeking, or risk-neutral strategies. Decision trees are widely used in business applications such as supplier selection, product launches, credit approval, research and development investments, healthcare planning, and public policy decisions.
