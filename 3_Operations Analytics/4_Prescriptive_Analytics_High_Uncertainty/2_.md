# Operations Analytics - Week 4, Session 2: Using Simulation with Decision Trees

This session extends the decision tree analysis from Session 1 by introducing a more realistic demand model. Previously, demand had only two possible outcomes: **5,000 units (weak market)** or **10,000 units (strong market)**. While simple, this did not fully capture real-world uncertainty. To model demand more accurately, the session combines **decision trees** with **simulation**.

The business scenario remains the same. IDEA must choose between two suppliers for its Krusbar tent:

* **Supplier S**: Capacity = 5,000 units, Fixed Cost = €0, Unit Cost = €120
* **Supplier P**: Capacity = 10,000 units, Fixed Cost = €50,000, Unit Cost = €100

The selling price remains **€150 per tent**. There is still a 50% chance of a weak market and a 50% chance of a strong market. However, demand within each market condition is now uncertain.

### Enhanced Demand Model

Instead of a single demand value:

**Weak Market**

* Demand follows a Uniform Distribution between 2,000 and 8,000 units.

**Strong Market**

* Demand follows a Uniform Distribution between 6,000 and 14,000 units.

A Uniform Distribution means every value within the specified range is equally likely to occur.

### Profit Calculation

Let:

* (Q) = Order Quantity
* (D) = Demand

Since sales cannot exceed either demand or inventory, actual sales equal:

[
\min(D,Q)
]

Revenue becomes:

[
150 \times \min(D,Q)
]

Profit is calculated as:

[
Profit = -Fixed\ Cost - Unit\ Cost \times Q + 150 \times \min(D,Q)
]

This formula works for all demand levels and supplier choices.

### Using Simulation

Because demand can take thousands of possible values, representing every outcome in a decision tree would be impractical. Instead, simulation is used.

The process is:

1. Generate random demand values from the appropriate Uniform Distribution.
2. Calculate profit for each demand sample.
3. Repeat many times (1,000 simulations).
4. Compute the average profit.

The average simulated profit becomes the estimated **expected value** for that branch of the decision tree.

### Simulation Results

After running 1,000 simulations, the estimated profits were:

| Supplier   | Market Condition | Expected Profit (€) |
| ---------- | ---------------- | ------------------- |
| Supplier S | Weak Market      | 42,405              |
| Supplier S | Strong Market    | 150,000             |
| Supplier P | Weak Market      | -293,391            |
| Supplier P | Strong Market    | 306,540             |

These values replace the complex demand branches in the decision tree.

### Calculating Overall Expected Value

For Supplier S:

[
0.5(42,405)+0.5(150,000)
=96,203
]

Expected Profit ≈ **€96,200**

For Supplier P:

[
0.5(-293,391)+0.5(306,540)
=6,575
]

Expected Profit ≈ **€6,575**

No Supplier:

[
0
]

Expected Profit = **€0**

### Best Decision

Comparing expected profits:

* No Supplier = €0
* Supplier S = €96,200
* Supplier P = €6,575

Therefore, the **expected-value maximizing decision is Supplier S**. Although Supplier P has lower unit costs, the risk of over-ordering and unsold inventory in weak-demand scenarios greatly reduces its attractiveness.

### Key Takeaways

Decision trees work well when outcomes are simple and discrete, but they become difficult to manage when uncertainty has many possible values. Simulation solves this problem by estimating the expected value of complex event nodes. By combining simulation with decision trees, managers can evaluate realistic uncertainty while still using the structured decision-making framework of decision trees. This integration provides a powerful approach for making business decisions under uncertainty.
