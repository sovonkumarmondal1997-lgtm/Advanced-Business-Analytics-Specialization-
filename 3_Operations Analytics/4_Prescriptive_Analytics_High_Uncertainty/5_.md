# Operations Analytics - Week 4 Review: Decision Trees and Decision Strategies

This session explains how **decision trees** help organizations make decisions under uncertainty. The case study involves a patent infringement dispute between two companies. The goal is to determine whether pursuing litigation is worthwhile by considering possible outcomes, probabilities, costs, and rewards. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

A decision tree consists of three components. **Decision nodes** (squares) represent choices that decision-makers control. **Event nodes** (circles) represent uncertain events with known probabilities. **Outcome nodes** (triangles) represent final payoffs or losses. By combining decisions, uncertainties, and financial consequences, a decision tree provides a structured way to evaluate complex business problems. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

The case study follows a three-stage litigation process. In Stage 1, the court decides whether the patent is valid. If the patent is invalid, the case ends. If it is valid, the company can choose whether to continue. In Stage 2, the court decides whether infringement occurred. If infringement is not proven, the case ends. If infringement is proven, the company can choose whether to proceed to Stage 3. In Stage 3, the court determines the amount of damages awarded. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

The estimated probabilities are as follows:

- Patent valid: 2/3 probability
- Patent invalid: 1/3 probability
- Infringement proven: 1/4 probability
- No infringement: 3/4 probability
- Damage awards: $20M, $30M, $40M, and $50M, each with probability 1/4

The litigation costs are:

- Stage 1: $3M
- Stage 2: $5M
- Stage 3: $1M

To calculate outcomes, all costs incurred along a path are subtracted from any damages received. For example, reaching Stage 3 and receiving $20M damages results in a net payoff of $11M ($20M − $3M − $5M − $1M). Similarly, damage awards of $30M, $40M, and $50M produce net payoffs of $21M, $31M, and $41M respectively. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

The session then introduces three decision-making strategies. The first is the **Maxi-Min Strategy**, which is risk-averse. It focuses on maximizing the worst possible outcome. By working backward through the tree and always considering the minimum outcome at each uncertainty point, the best choice is not to begin litigation at all, resulting in a payoff of $0 instead of risking losses. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

The second is the **Maxi-Max Strategy**, which is risk-seeking. It focuses on maximizing the best possible outcome regardless of risk. Since the highest possible payoff is $41M, the company should proceed through all litigation stages to keep that opportunity available. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

The third is the **Expected Value Strategy**, which is risk-neutral. Expected value is calculated by multiplying each outcome by its probability and summing the results. Working backward through the tree, the final expected value of initiating litigation is approximately -$0.67M. Since not litigating produces an expected value of $0, the optimal decision is again not to litigate. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

The key lesson is that decision trees combine probabilities, costs, rewards, and choices into a single framework. Different decision-makers may choose different strategies depending on whether they prioritize minimizing risk, maximizing reward, or maximizing expected value. In this case, both the risk-minimizing and expected-value approaches recommend avoiding litigation, while the reward-maximizing approach recommends pursuing the case through all stages. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}
