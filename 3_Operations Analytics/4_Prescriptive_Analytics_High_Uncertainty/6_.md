# Operations Analytics - Week 4 Advanced Session: Converting Nonlinear Optimization into Linear Optimization

This advanced session explains how a **nonlinear optimization problem containing a MIN function** can be transformed into a **linear optimization problem**. The example uses IDEA's supplier selection problem from Week 4, where the company must decide the optimal order quantity of tents under uncertain demand. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

In the original problem, IDEA chooses an order quantity \(Q\) between 4,000 and 10,000 units. The selling price is €150 per unit, the unit cost is €100, and there is a fixed supplier fee of €100,000. Demand is uncertain and follows a Uniform Distribution between 6,000 and 14,000 units. Profit is calculated as:

\[
Profit = 150 \times \min(D,Q) - 100Q - 100,000
\]

where:

- \(D\) = Demand
- \(Q\) = Order Quantity
- \(\min(D,Q)\) = Actual units sold

The MIN function is necessary because sales cannot exceed either demand or available inventory. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

The problem is first implemented in Excel using randomly generated demand samples. For each demand sample, revenue, cost, and profit are calculated. The average profit across all samples becomes the objective function. Using Solver with the **GRG Nonlinear** method, the optimal order quantity is approximately **7,884 units**, producing an average profit of about **€240,000** for the simulated demand scenarios. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

The session then explains why the MIN function creates a nonlinear model. If sales are plotted against demand, the graph follows demand until demand reaches the order quantity. Beyond that point, sales remain constant because inventory limits further sales. This creates a sharp corner, or **kink**, in the graph. Such kinks make optimization models nonlinear and harder to solve efficiently. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

To eliminate the MIN function, a new decision variable is introduced:

\[
S_i = \text{Units Sold for Demand Sample } i
\]

Instead of defining sales using MIN, two constraints are added:

\[
S_i \le D_i
\]

\[
S_i \le Q
\]

These constraints ensure that sales can never exceed either demand or inventory. The profit equation becomes:

\[
Profit_i = 150S_i - 100Q - 100,000
\]

which is completely linear. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

Although the constraints only require sales to be less than or equal to demand and inventory, maximizing profit automatically pushes each \(S_i\) as high as possible. Therefore, in the optimal solution:

\[
S_i = \min(D_i,Q)
\]

even though the MIN function no longer appears in the model. This clever modeling technique allows the nonlinear behavior to be reproduced using only linear equations and constraints. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

The linear version is then solved using Excel Solver's **Simplex LP** method. After optimization, the solution remains essentially identical:

- Optimal Order Quantity ≈ 7,884 units
- Average Profit ≈ €240,000

This confirms that the linear formulation produces the same business decision while being more stable and easier for optimization software to solve. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

The key lesson is that many nonlinear optimization problems can be transformed into linear models by introducing additional decision variables and constraints. Linear models are generally faster, more reliable, and easier to solve, making this a valuable modeling technique in operations analytics and optimization. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}
