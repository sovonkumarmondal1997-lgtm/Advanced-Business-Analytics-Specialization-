# Operations Analytics - Week 3, Session 3: Interpreting Simulation Results and Using Histograms

This session focuses on understanding simulation results, evaluating their accuracy, and visualizing probability distributions using **histograms**. The main objective is to determine how reliable simulation estimates are and how the number of simulation runs affects their precision.

The wireless data plan example is used again. In the previous session, a simulation with **10 runs** was created to estimate the reward and risk associated with the new data plan. The simulation generated random monthly data usage values from a Normal distribution with:

* Mean = 23 GB
* Standard Deviation = 5 GB

Each simulated usage value was converted into a monthly payment using the payment formula. The resulting sample was then used to estimate reward and risk. However, because only 10 random observations were generated, the estimates were not very accurate. The sample mean of usage was around 25 GB instead of the true value of 23 GB, and the sample standard deviation was about 8 GB instead of 5 GB. Similarly, the estimated reward was about **$253** and the estimated risk was about **$92**, which may differ substantially from the true values.

To improve accuracy, the simulation was expanded to **1,000 runs**. With a much larger sample, the simulated data usage distribution more closely matched the true distribution. The sample mean became approximately **23.3 GB** and the sample standard deviation approximately **4.9 GB**, both very close to the actual values of 23 and 5. The corresponding estimates for the new data plan were:

* **Reward (Expected Monthly Payment) ≈ $220**
* **Risk (Standard Deviation of Payments) ≈ $58**

This demonstrates an important simulation principle: **the more simulation runs performed, the more accurate and stable the estimates become**. Long simulations provide results that are much closer to the true probability distribution than short simulations.

The session also discusses the role of a **random seed**, which determines the sequence of random numbers generated during simulation. Short simulations can produce very different results when different seed values are used because the sample size is small. However, in long simulations with 1,000 runs, changing the seed has very little effect on the final estimates. Therefore, when simulations contain many runs, the choice of seed becomes largely unimportant.

To visualize simulation results, the session introduces **histograms**. A histogram is a frequency chart that shows how often simulated values fall within specified ranges called **bins**. Histograms provide a visual representation of the probability distribution generated by the simulation.

A histogram of the simulated data usage values showed the familiar bell-shaped pattern of a Normal distribution. A histogram of the monthly payment values revealed a very different shape because the payment formula is nonlinear. One particularly important insight was that approximately **250 out of 1,000 simulations produced a payment of exactly $160**, indicating roughly a **25% probability** that monthly usage would stay below 20 GB and trigger only the fixed monthly fee. This type of insight would be difficult to obtain without simulation and visualization.

The session concludes by reinforcing the three-step framework for decision-making under uncertainty:

1. Define appropriate **reward and risk measures**.
2. Use **simulation** to estimate those measures.
3. Use **optimization** to select the best decision while balancing reward and risk.

The key lesson is that simulation converts uncertain inputs into distributions of possible outcomes, while histograms help managers understand those distributions visually. Together, they provide a powerful foundation for making informed decisions in uncertain business environments.
