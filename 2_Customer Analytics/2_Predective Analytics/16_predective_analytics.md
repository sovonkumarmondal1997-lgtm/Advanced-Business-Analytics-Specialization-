# Customer-Based Corporate Valuation (CBCV) Case Study: Farfetch IPO

This case study demonstrates how Customer-Based Corporate Valuation (CBCV) can estimate the value of a company using customer metrics rather than relying solely on traditional financial statements. The example focuses on Farfetch, an online luxury marketplace, showing how Buy-Till-You-Die (BTYD) models can be reverse-engineered from publicly disclosed customer metrics to estimate future company value. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## The Core Idea

When companies prepare for an Initial Public Offering (IPO), they publish documents containing operational and customer-related metrics. While most investors view these numbers as descriptive statistics, CBCV treats them as inputs for predictive models.

The goal is to answer:

- How many customers will the company acquire?
- How long will those customers stay?
- How often will they purchase?
- How much will they spend?

By forecasting these behaviors, it becomes possible to estimate future revenue, cash flow, and corporate value. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Reverse Engineering Customer Behavior

A key insight of CBCV is that detailed customer transaction logs are not always required.

Companies often disclose aggregate customer metrics such as:

- Active customers.
- Total orders.
- Repeat customer percentages.
- Revenue-related measures.

Using these public metrics, analysts can reverse-engineer the parameters of BTYD models and reconstruct customer behavior patterns almost as if they had access to the original transaction-level data. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

## The Farfetch Example

Farfetch provided enough customer metrics in its IPO filing to allow analysts to estimate:

- Customer acquisition rates.
- Customer retention patterns.
- Purchase frequency.
- Spending behavior.

The model was not designed specifically to fit Farfetch's data. Instead, analysts first selected a behavioral framework:

1. Acquisition Model.
2. Buy-Till-You-Die (BTYD) Model.
3. Spend Model.

Only after choosing the framework was the company data used to estimate the parameters.

This approach emphasizes theory-driven modeling rather than simply fitting patterns in historical data. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## The Three Components of the Model

### 1. Acquisition Model

Forecasts:

- How many new customers will be acquired.
- Future customer growth.

### 2. BTYD Model

Forecasts:

- Customer retention.
- Customer dropout.
- Purchase frequency.
- Future transaction activity.

### 3. Spend Model

Forecasts:

- Spending per transaction.
- Future customer revenue contribution.

Together, these models create a complete customer-driven revenue forecast. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

## Customer Cohort Analysis

The case study introduces the concept of a **Customer Cohort Chart (C3 Chart)**.

A cohort consists of customers acquired during the same period.

The chart tracks:

- Revenue generated by each cohort.
- How cohort value evolves over time.
- How quickly customer spending declines or persists.

By examining multiple cohorts simultaneously, analysts can understand long-term customer behavior and identify retention patterns that drive company value. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

## Understanding Retention Curves

One of the most important outputs of the BTYD model is the retention curve.

The retention curve shows:

- What percentage of customers remain active over time.
- How quickly customers become inactive.
- Differences between customer segments.

### Typical Shape

The Farfetch case displayed:

1. A sharp initial decline.
2. A gradual leveling off.

This pattern reveals **customer heterogeneity**.

#### Early Drop-Off Customers

Some customers:

- Purchase once.
- Never return.

These customers create the steep initial decline.

#### Loyal Long-Term Customers

Other customers:

- Purchase repeatedly.
- Stay active for many years.

These customers create the long tail of the retention curve.

This long-term customer group often represents the most valuable asset of the business. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

## Revealing Hidden Customer Assets

An important advantage of CBCV is its ability to uncover information not explicitly disclosed.

The company may not report:

- Retention rates by cohort.
- Lifetime value distributions.
- Future customer spending.
- Customer longevity.

However, once the model accurately fits observable customer metrics, analysts can infer these hidden behavioral characteristics.

In other words:

Observed Metrics → Model → Hidden Customer Insights

This allows organizations and investors to better understand the underlying strength of a customer base. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

## From Customer Behavior to Stock Valuation

Once customer acquisition, retention, purchasing, and spending are forecasted, the next step is straightforward:

Customer Forecasts → Revenue Forecasts → Cash Flow Forecasts → Company Valuation

The model converts customer behavior into financial outcomes.

This creates a direct link between:

- Customer analytics.
- Corporate finance.
- Investment decisions.

Rather than valuing a company through accounting numbers alone, CBCV values the company through the future behavior of its customers. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

## Why This Matters

The case study demonstrates several important lessons:

### Customer Metrics Are Strategic Assets

Metrics such as:

- Active customers.
- Purchase frequency.
- Retention.
- Cohort performance.

contain valuable information about future business performance.

### Public Data Can Be Powerful

Even limited disclosures can reveal significant insights when combined with behavioral models.

### Customer Value Drives Company Value

A company's future revenue ultimately depends on:

- Acquiring customers.
- Retaining customers.
- Increasing customer spending.

Understanding these behaviors provides a strong foundation for valuation. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

## CBCV Beyond Farfetch

The same framework can be applied across industries, including:

- E-commerce.
- Subscription businesses.
- SaaS companies.
- Marketplaces.
- Retail businesses.

As long as customer behavior can be modeled, the company can potentially be valued using CBCV principles. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

## Key Takeaway

The Farfetch case study shows how Customer-Based Corporate Valuation transforms customer analytics into a finance tool. By combining customer acquisition models, Buy-Till-You-Die models, and spending models, analysts can forecast customer behavior, estimate future revenues, and determine corporate value. The most important insight is that a company's true value often lies in its customer relationships, and those relationships can be quantified even when only limited customer metrics are publicly available. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}
