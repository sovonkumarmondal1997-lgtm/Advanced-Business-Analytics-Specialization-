# Descriptive Data Collection and Survey Design:

Descriptive data collection is used to answer factual questions about customers, such as who they are, what they buy, how much they spend, and how they can be segmented. It relies on quantitative data and can be collected through two main approaches: **active data collection** and **unobtrusive data collection**. Active data collection mainly includes surveys and customer self-reports. Surveys are one of the most widely used tools in customer analytics because they help organizations gather information about customer attitudes, preferences, satisfaction levels, purchase behavior, and market segments. Mobile surveys have further improved data collection by capturing customer feedback in real time and within the context of a purchase or location. However, excessive surveying can lead to survey fatigue, reducing response quality. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Types of Survey Questions

### Itemized Category Scale
Respondents select from predefined categories such as "very satisfied" to "very dissatisfied." These questions are easy to understand and balanced, but respondents may use different reference points when answering, leading to inconsistent interpretations.

### Comparative Scale
Respondents compare one option against another specific benchmark. This removes ambiguity about the comparison standard but may hide the fact that both options are poor overall.

### Ranking Questions
Respondents rank multiple attributes from most important to least important. This helps identify priorities but becomes difficult when too many options are presented because respondents must make numerous comparisons.

### Paired Comparison Questions
Respondents choose between two or three alternatives. This closely resembles real-world decision-making and helps identify customer preferences. However, respondents may dislike all available options, and too many brands or features can make comparisons difficult.

### Likert Scale
The most common survey format. Respondents indicate their level of agreement or disagreement with various statements. It efficiently collects large amounts of attitude and opinion data.

### Continuous Scale
Respondents continuously adjust a rating while interacting with content such as advertisements, videos, or debates. This helps measure changing preferences in real time. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Survey Quality: Validity and Reliability

A good survey must be both **valid** and **reliable**.

### Validity
Validity measures whether a survey predicts meaningful business outcomes. For example, if customer satisfaction scores can predict future profits or customer loyalty, the survey has strong predictive validity.

### Reliability
Reliability measures consistency over time. A reliable survey produces similar results when repeated under similar conditions. One common measure is test-retest reliability, which checks whether responses remain stable across multiple measurements. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

## Advantages and Limitations of Surveys

Surveys are relatively low-cost, easy to implement, and effective for learning about customers. However, designing unbiased questions is challenging, selecting the right respondents is critical, and surveys may not work well for products that require hands-on experience before evaluation. In such cases, methods like focus groups may be more appropriate. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## Key Takeaway

Descriptive analytics depends heavily on surveys to collect structured customer data. Choosing the right survey type, asking appropriate questions, and ensuring validity and reliability are essential for obtaining useful insights that support business decisions.
