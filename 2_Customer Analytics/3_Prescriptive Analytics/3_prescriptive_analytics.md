# Prescriptive Analytics: Profit Maximization and the Marginal Revenue = Marginal Cost Principle

In many business situations, maximizing revenue is not the ultimate goal. Companies care about **profit**, which is the money left after paying all production costs. Prescriptive analytics helps companies determine the price that maximizes profit by considering both revenue and cost. This introduces an important extension to optimization: accounting for parameters such as production costs when making decisions. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Revenue vs. Profit

Revenue and profit are not the same.

### Revenue

Money generated from selling products.

:contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

### Profit

Money remaining after subtracting production costs.

:contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

A company can generate high revenue and still lose money if production costs are too high. Therefore, maximizing revenue does not necessarily maximize profit. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## Introducing Costs into the Model

Suppose each product costs **$2 to produce**.

Now every pricing decision affects:

- Revenue earned from customers.
- Cost of producing the products sold.

At very low prices:

- Demand is high.
- Revenue per unit is low.
- Total costs may exceed revenue.

In this situation, the company loses money even though it sells many units. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

## Finding the Profit-Maximizing Price

To calculate profit:

1. Estimate demand at different prices.
2. Calculate revenue.
3. Calculate total production costs.
4. Subtract costs from revenue.

When this analysis is performed, the profit curve differs from the revenue curve.

### Revenue-Maximizing Price

Approximately:

**$5.50**

### Profit-Maximizing Price

Approximately:

**$6.50**

This demonstrates that the optimal profit price is higher than the optimal revenue price because costs must be covered. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

## Understanding the Trade-Off

### Increasing Price from a Low Level

When price increases slightly:

- Revenue increases.
- Quantity decreases.
- Production costs decrease.

The combined effect increases profit.

Example:

- Revenue gain = $3.49
- Cost reduction = $0.90
- Total profit increase = $4.39

Profit rises when the price increases. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

### Increasing Price from a High Level

When price becomes too high:

- Revenue decreases significantly.
- Costs continue to decrease.
- Revenue loss becomes larger than cost savings.

Example:

- Revenue loss = $1.01
- Cost reduction = $0.90
- Net profit change = -$0.11

Profit falls when the price increases further. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

## The Key Optimization Question

How do we know when to stop increasing the price?

The answer is:

**Increase the price until additional profit becomes zero.**

At that point, moving the price either higher or lower will reduce profit.

This leads to one of the most important principles in economics and prescriptive analytics. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

## Marginal Revenue = Marginal Cost Principle

The optimal profit occurs when:

:contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

This is known as the **MR = MC Principle**. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

### Marginal Revenue (MR)

The additional revenue generated from selling one more unit.

### Marginal Cost (MC)

The additional cost incurred from producing one more unit.

When these two values become equal:

- Additional sales no longer increase profit.
- Additional production no longer creates value.
- Profit reaches its maximum level.

This is the point where the company should stop adjusting its price. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

## Why MR = MC Works

Consider three situations:

### MR > MC

- Additional sales generate more revenue than cost.
- Profit increases.
- Continue producing and selling more.

### MR < MC

- Additional sales cost more than they generate.
- Profit decreases.
- Reduce production or raise price.

### MR = MC

- Additional sales neither increase nor decrease profit.
- Profit is maximized.

This is the optimal operating point. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

## General Prescriptive Analytics Framework

The profit maximization problem still follows the same three-part structure:

### Objective

Maximize profit.

### Action

Set the product price.

### Model

Use the demand curve along with cost information to predict how pricing affects profit.

Optimization then identifies the price that generates the highest profit. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

## Key Takeaway

Profit maximization extends revenue optimization by incorporating production costs into the decision-making process. While the revenue-maximizing price in the example is approximately **$5.50**, the profit-maximizing price is approximately **$6.50** because costs must be considered. The most important principle for finding optimal profit is the **Marginal Revenue = Marginal Cost (MR = MC) Rule**, which states that profit is maximized when the additional revenue from selling one more unit exactly equals the additional cost of producing that unit. This principle is one of the most widely used optimization rules in business, economics, and prescriptive analytics. :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14}
