# The Golden Age of Marketing and Customer Analytics

This lecture explains how marketing evolved from broad, store-level decision making into a highly data-driven science focused on understanding and optimizing individual customer behavior. The central idea is that modern marketing succeeds by collecting detailed customer data, analyzing it, and making decisions one customer at a time. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Why Marketing Has Become a Science

Marketing today resembles a scientific process because organizations can:

1. Collect data.
2. Explore data.
3. Build predictive models.
4. Optimize decisions.
5. Measure outcomes.

Technology, data, and statistics have transformed marketing from intuition-based decision making into evidence-based decision making. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## The Customer-Centric View of Profit

Traditional finance defines profit as:

Profit = Revenue − Cost

Customer analytics approaches the problem differently.

Instead of focusing on products, it focuses on customers:

- How much revenue does each customer generate?
- What does it cost to serve that customer?
- What profit does each customer contribute?

The organization then adds the profit contributions of all customers.

### Why This Matters

This perspective reveals that:

- Some customers generate high profits.
- Some customers generate little value.
- Some customers may actually destroy value.

Organizations can therefore:

- Retain valuable customers.
- Acquire more high-value customers.
- Reduce investment in unprofitable customers.

The guiding principle is:

**Make profit one customer at a time.** :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

# What Is Customer Analytics?

Customer analytics is defined as:

> The collection, management, analysis, and strategic use of granular customer data to improve business decisions.

The definition contains four key components:

### 1. Collection

Gathering customer information from various sources.

### 2. Management

Storing and organizing data in databases and systems.

### 3. Analysis

Extracting insights from raw information.

### 4. Strategic Leverage

Using those insights to improve business outcomes.

Customer analytics is not merely about having data; it is about turning data into decisions. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

# Characteristics of Customer Analytics

## Granular

Customer analytics focuses on individuals rather than aggregates.

Instead of asking:

- What are total sales?

It asks:

- What did each customer buy?

Individual-level analysis enables personalization and more precise targeting. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

## Behavioral

The focus is on actual behavior.

Examples:

- Purchases
- Website visits
- Product usage
- Donations
- Subscription activity

Past behavior is generally a stronger predictor of future behavior than survey responses or stated intentions. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

## Forward-Looking

The goal is prediction.

Examples:

- What will customers buy next?
- Who will churn?
- Who is most valuable?
- What revenue will future customers generate?

Customer analytics focuses on future actions rather than merely describing the past. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

## Multi-Platform

Customer information comes from many channels:

- Online interactions
- Offline purchases
- Mobile devices
- Surveys
- CRM systems

Combining these sources is known as **data fusion**. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

## Broadly Applicable

The term "customer" extends beyond buyers.

Examples include:

- Website visitors
- Travelers
- Blood donors
- Physicians
- Patients
- Business clients

Customer analytics can be applied wherever individuals interact with organizations. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

## Multidisciplinary

Customer analytics combines:

- Marketing
- Statistics
- Computer Science
- Information Systems
- Operations Research
- Business Analytics

Its techniques work across industries and business functions. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

# Evolution of Marketing Data

## 1950s: Store-Level Data

In the 1950s, businesses mainly collected information at the store level.

### Questions Businesses Could Answer

- How do prices affect sales?
- How effective are coupons?
- How do promotions affect store performance?
- How do regions differ?

### Limitation

Businesses could not identify individual customers.

Analysis was performed at the store level, not the customer level. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

---

## 1960s–1970s: Direct Mail Era

Direct mail introduced household-level marketing.

Companies knew:

- Which catalogs they sent
- Which households received them
- Which households responded

### New Capabilities

- Target marketing
- Controlled experiments
- Personalized promotions
- Advertising testing

### Example Questions

- How often should catalogs be sent?
- What timing is optimal?
- Which advertising message performs best?
- How does catalog size affect purchases?

This period laid the foundation for modern customer analytics. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

---

## 1980s: Store Scanners and Loyalty Programs

The introduction of barcode scanners transformed marketing.

Businesses could now record:

- Individual purchases
- Transaction histories
- Product-level buying behavior

### Loyalty Programs

When customers use:

- Loyalty cards
- Credit cards

Businesses can connect purchases across time.

This creates a **panel dataset**, allowing firms to track customer behavior longitudinally. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

### New Capabilities

- Customer tracking
- Personalized coupons
- Targeted promotions
- Purchase history analysis

This marked the beginning of modern customer analytics. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

---

## 1990s–2000s: Internet Revolution

The internet changed marketing not merely by creating online sales, but by creating massive behavioral datasets.

### What Internet Data Reveals

- Pages viewed
- Products considered
- Search behavior
- Advertisement exposure
- Browsing patterns

### Why This Matters

Purchases tell firms:

- What customers bought.

Internet behavior tells firms:

- What customers considered buying.

This distinction is crucial.

Low sales can occur because:

1. Customers considered the product and rejected it.
2. Customers never considered the product.

These require completely different business strategies. :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14}

# Data Fusion: The Modern Analytics Requirement

Modern firms need to combine:

- Online browsing data
- Purchase data
- Loyalty program data
- Advertising exposure data

### Example

A customer may:

1. Research online.
2. Visit a store.
3. Purchase later online.

Or:

1. Research online.
2. Buy in a physical store.

Without linking online and offline data, firms lose visibility into the customer journey. :contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}
