# New Data Sources in Marketing Analytics

This lecture focuses on how modern technology has created entirely new types of customer data that allow companies to understand customer behavior at a much deeper level than traditional sales data. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

---

# Traditional Marketing Data

Historically, companies mainly had access to:

- TV advertising data
- Online advertising data
- Sales transaction data
- Scanner data at checkout counters

These datasets only showed:

**What customers bought**

but not

**How they arrived at that decision.** :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

---

# Modern Marketing Data Revolution

Today companies can collect many additional forms of customer data.

Examples include:

### 1. Purchase Intentions

Before entering a store:

- What did the customer plan to buy?

After shopping:

- What did the customer actually buy?

Comparing the two reveals:

- Planned purchases
- Impulse purchases
- Influence of store layout

This helps companies understand how shopping behavior changes inside the store. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

---

### 2. Shopper Path Data

Companies can track:

- Where customers walk
- Which aisles they visit
- How long they stay in each section

This is called:

**Shopper Path Analytics**. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

---

### 3. Eye Tracking Data

Companies can measure:

- Which products customers look at
- How long they look
- Which products are ignored

Important insight:

A customer cannot buy a product they never notice. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

---

### 4. Purchase Data

Traditional checkout data still remains important.

Now companies combine:

- Purchase data
- Path data
- Eye tracking
- Customer intentions

to obtain a complete view of customer behavior. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

# Path Tracker Project

Researchers created a system called **Path Tracker**.

Technology:

- Small devices attached to shopping carts
- Wireless signals transmitted through the store
- Customer location identified within approximately one foot

This allowed researchers to observe exactly how shoppers move through stores. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

---

# Modern Version: Mobile Phone Tracking

Today the same idea is even more powerful.

Since customers carry smartphones:

- Location can be tracked continuously
- Movement patterns can be analyzed
- Physical store behavior becomes measurable

This creates valuable geospatial customer data. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

---

# Website Analytics Equivalent

The same concept applies online.

Companies track:

- Which pages you visit
- Visit sequence
- Time spent on each page
- Navigation behavior

This is essentially digital path tracking. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

# How Customers Actually Move Through Stores

Many managers assume customers move systematically:

- Up one aisle
- Down the next aisle
- Through the entire store

Analytics shows this is wrong. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

---

## Traverse

A customer:

- Enters one side of an aisle
- Exits through the opposite side

This is called a **Traverse**. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

---

## Zig-Zag

Customer:

- Goes through one aisle
- Crosses over
- Moves through another aisle

This is called a **Zig-Zag** pattern. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

---

## Excursion

Most common behavior:

- Customer enters an aisle
- Travels part way down
- Returns from the same side

This is called an **Excursion**. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

---

# Important Discovery

Most customers:

- Do not visit every aisle
- Do not cover the entire store

Research found:

**Customers visit only about 25% of a store during a typical shopping trip.**

This means:

**Approximately 75% of the store is never visited during a given trip.** :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

---

# Store Heatmaps

Analytics creates heatmaps showing traffic intensity.

### Hot Areas (Red)

High customer traffic:

- Outer perimeter
- Main walkways
- End caps

### Cold Areas (Blue)

Low customer traffic:

- Middle sections of aisles
- Interior locations

These areas receive very little customer attention. :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14}

---

# Racetrack Concept

The outer perimeter of a supermarket is called the:

**Racetrack**

Characteristics:

- Highest traffic area
- Most customer visibility
- Premium shelf locations

This explains why manufacturers pay more for:

- End-cap displays
- Perimeter shelf space
- High-traffic locations

Because customers are far more likely to see those products. :contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}

---

# Business Application

Suppose a cereal company's sales are low.

Traditional thinking:

> Customers dislike the product.

Analytics may reveal:

> Customers never walk near the shelf where the product is located.

The solution becomes:

- Move product location
- Purchase better shelf space
- Improve visibility

instead of changing the product itself. :contentReference[oaicite:16]{index=16}

# Why Customer-Level Tracking Matters

Older methods:

- Human observation
- Manual recording
- Security cameras

Problems:

- Expensive
- Not scalable
- Error-prone

Modern analytics allows automatic tracking of:

- Individual customer movements
- Browsing behavior
- Purchase activity

and links all information together. :contentReference[oaicite:17]{index=17}

# Key Takeaways

### Traditional Analytics

Focused on:

- What customers bought

### Modern Analytics

Focuses on:

- What customers intended to buy
- Where customers moved
- What they looked at
- What they purchased

### Major Insights

1. Most customers visit only about 25% of a store.
2. The store perimeter receives the highest traffic.
3. Shelf placement strongly influences sales.
4. Customer path data can explain poor product performance.
5. Location analytics helps optimize store layout and merchandising.

These techniques are widely used today in retail analytics, customer analytics, location intelligence, geospatial analytics, and marketing science. :contentReference[oaicite:18]{index=18}
