# The Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP)

The **Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP)** is a famous optimization problem.

Question:

> What is the shortest route a salesperson can take to visit all required cities and return?

Businesses use the same idea inside stores:

> What is the shortest path a shopper could take to collect all purchased products and check out? :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

---

# Shopper Efficiency Analysis

Researchers compared:

### Shopper A

- Takes the shortest path
- Goes directly to products
- Leaves quickly

### Shopper B

- Wanders around
- Explores additional aisles
- Takes a longer route

Question:

> Which shopper generates more value for the retailer? :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

---

# Surprising Finding

Most shoppers are not efficient.

Research found:

### Average TSP Optimality = 28%

Meaning:

- Only 28% of customer movement is necessary.
- Roughly 72–75% of movement is extra wandering. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

---

# Types of Shopper Behavior

Researchers categorized shoppers based on:

### 1. Order Deviation

Did shoppers visit product locations in the optimal order?

Example:

Optimal:

A → B → C

Actual:

C → A → B

This creates order deviation. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

---

### 2. Travel Deviation

How much extra movement occurred?

Example:

Instead of walking directly between locations, the shopper:

- Explored
- Wandered
- Made detours

This creates travel deviation. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

---

# Most Valuable Customers

The most valuable shoppers were:

### Group 4

Characteristics:

- Highest travel deviation
- Highest order deviation
- Most wandering behavior

Result:

- Purchased approximately 10 items per visit

Compared with:

### Group 1

- Most efficient shoppers

Result:

- Purchased roughly half as many items. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

---

# Key Business Insight

Retailers should NOT always optimize for speed.

Why?

Because shoppers who spend more time exploring often:

- Discover more products
- Make more impulse purchases
- Spend more money

Therefore:

> More wandering can generate more sales. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

# Shelf Placement Optimization

Question:

> Where should a product be placed on a shelf?

Eye-tracking analytics provided the answer.

---

## Best Shelf Height

Products perform best at approximately:

### 5 feet 6 inches

Reason:

- Close to average female eye level.
- Most supermarket shoppers are female.

People naturally prefer looking:

- Straight ahead
- Not upward
- Not downward. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

---

## Left Side vs Right Side

Research found:

### Left side performs better.

Reason:

People read:

Left → Right

Therefore shoppers visually scan shelves from left to right. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

---

# Eye Tracking and Marketing Effectiveness

Researchers measured:

### Fixations

How many times a shopper looked at a product.

Categories:

- 0 views
- 1 view
- 2+ views

Then they measured:

- Recall
- Consideration
- Purchase behavior. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

---

# Finding 1: Recall

More views →

Higher recall

Customers remember products better when they see them multiple times. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

---

# Finding 2: Consideration

More views →

Higher consideration

Customers become more likely to think about purchasing the product. :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14}

---

# Finding 3: Purchase

More views →

Slightly higher purchase probability

The effect exists but is weaker than recall and consideration. :contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}

---

# Marketing Funnel Insight

The process works as:

View Product
↓
Recall Product
↓
Consider Product
↓
Purchase Product

Analytics confirmed that visibility strongly influences the first three stages. :contentReference[oaicite:16]{index=16}

# Planned vs Unplanned Purchases

Before entering stores, shoppers were asked:

> What do you plan to buy?

Researchers then compared plans with actual purchases.

Results:

### 60% of purchases were unplanned

Customers entered the store with no intention of buying these items. :contentReference[oaicite:17]{index=17}

---

### 40% of planned purchases were never bought

Customers intended to buy them but left without purchasing them. :contentReference[oaicite:18]{index=18}
